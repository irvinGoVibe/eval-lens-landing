# Research: "Hundreds of decks, one ranked shortlist"

Article publish date (backdated): **2026-05-09**
Hard rule applied: every source below was published **on or before early May 2026**. Newest source used: 2026-02-17 (EIC results). Nothing from March–August 2026 is included.

## 1. Time per deck: the baseline (2015)

**Fact.** DocSend, with HBS professor Tom Eisenmann, analyzed 200+ pitch decks from companies that raised $360M in total. Investors spent an average of **3 minutes 44 seconds** on a deck. Same study: an average of ~40 investor meetings to close a round, ~12 weeks to close.
**Source.** TechCrunch, "Lessons From A Study Of Perfect Pitch Decks" — https://techcrunch.com/2015/06/08/lessons-from-a-study-of-perfect-pitch-decks-vcs-spend-an-average-of-3-minutes-44-seconds-on-them/
**Published.** 2015-06-08 `[direct]`
**Caveat.** DocSend's own co-founder called the sample small and not necessarily representative. Use as a historical anchor, not as a universal law.
**How to use in the playbook.** The opening reality check: "the deck you spent six weeks on gets under four minutes — and that was the *generous* era." Sets up the decline in fact #2.

## 2. Time per deck is shrinking (2022)

**Fact.** DocSend analysis of **320 pitch decks**: investors spent **24% less time** on decks in 2022 than in 2021, averaging **just under 3 minutes**. Decks that failed to raise were abandoned after **2 minutes 13 seconds**.
**Source.** TechCrunch, "The science of pitch decks" — https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/
**Published.** 2022-09-22 `[direct]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The core arithmetic of the piece: at ~2–3 minutes of real attention per deck, 300 applications = roughly 12–15 hours of pure reading before a single comparison is made. Attention is the constraint, not opinion.

## 3. Supply is rising while attention falls (2023)

**Fact.** DocSend pre-seed report covering 2022 and H1 2023 (200+ pre-seed startups): pitch deck **supply up 16% YoY** while **deck review time fell 11% YoY**. Founders contacted an average of **71 investors** and secured **46 meetings**. Successful decks averaged **16 slides**, unsuccessful ones **19**.
**Source.** PR Newswire / DocSend, "From Growth-At-All-Costs to Cost of Missing Out" — https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/from-growth-at-all-costs-to-cost-of-missing-out-docsend-pre-seed-report-shows-investor-shift-to-long-term-profitability-and-risk-aversion-301908433.html
**Published.** 2023-08-24 `[direct]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The scissors chart in words: more decks in, less time each. Any process that scales by "read harder" is already losing. Also justifies the playbook step "cap the read, standardize the extraction."

## 4. What the shrinking attention lands on (2024)

**Fact.** Dropbox DocSend annual reports covering **400+ pre-seed and seed startups**: VCs spent **40% more time on seed-stage Team slides** and **30% more time on pre-seed Team slides** in 2024 vs the prior year; investors prioritized traction, product and financials over market and competition sections.
**Source.** PR Newswire, "VCs prioritize people in an AI-heavy landscape" — https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/vcs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-dropbox-docsend-report-302334444.html
**Published.** 2024-12-18 `[snippet]`
**How to use in the playbook.** Tells you which fields the first pass must extract mechanically: team, traction, product, financials. Everything else is second-round reading, not screening material.

## 5. The funnel, with real denominators

**Fact.** Survey of **885 institutional VCs at 681 firms**. The median firm **considers ~200 companies and closes ~4 deals per year**. Per closed deal the firm **considers 101 opportunities**; **1 in 4** considered leads to a management meeting, **~1/3 of those** are reviewed at a partners meeting, **~half of those** go to due diligence, **~1/3 of those** get a term sheet, and **1.7 term sheets** are offered per close. Only **10%** of deal flow comes inbound from company management.
**Source.** Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan & Strebulaev, "How Do Venture Capitalists Make Decisions?" — NBER WP 22587, https://www.nber.org/papers/w22587 (published version: Journal of Financial Economics 135(1), 2020, 169–190)
**Published.** NBER working paper, September 2016; JFE version 2020 `[direct — Table 6 read from the PDF]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The one credible number for "how many things must die per yes": ~100 considered → 1 closed. The playbook's job is not to make the funnel narrower, it is to make the first 100→25 cut defensible and repeatable.

## 6. Volume and acceptance rates: the grant/accelerator analogue

**Fact.** EIC Accelerator, 1 October 2025 cut-off: **923 companies submitted full applications**, requesting nearly **€7 billion**; applicants from 32 countries; 74% asked for blended finance. Results published 17 February 2026: **121 of 923 (~13%) reached interview** and **61 were funded** — a full-application success rate of about **6.6%**.
**Sources.** European Innovation Council news, 2025-10-17 — https://eic.ec.europa.eu/news/strong-interest-eic-accelerator-funding-923-companies-applying-final-2025-cut-2025-10-17_en ; results coverage (Feb 2026) — https://www.strata.team/eic-accelerator-winners-october-2025-cut-off
**Published.** 2025-10-17 (application volume) and 2026-02-17 (results) `[snippet, official EC source for the 923 figure]`
**How to use in the playbook.** A dated, official, non-VC example that the "hundreds in, dozens out" shape is universal — grants, accelerators, competitions. Also gives a clean two-gate structure to mirror: mass screen → interview shortlist → decision.

## 7. Accelerator acceptance rates

**Fact.** Y Combinator's acceptance rate sits around **1%**; Techstars programs around **1–2%**. Application pools grew during the downturn even as cohorts shrank (YC cut cohort size ~40% from peak in summer 2022). There is **no established index of acceptance rates** across the accelerator ecosystem.
**Source.** Crunchbase News, "Down Times Look Like Up Times For Accelerator Applications" — https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/accelerators-incubators-layoffs-yc-nextview/
**Published.** 2023-05-15 `[direct]`
**Caveat.** Exact per-batch application counts are not officially published by YC. Do not state a precise YC application number in the article — say "roughly 1% get in" and cite this.
**How to use in the playbook.** Frames the stakes for the reviewer: at 1%, 99 of every 100 decisions are rejections, and the cost of a sloppy rejection is invisible. That is exactly why the process, not the taste, has to carry the weight.

## 8. Reviewers of the same applications do not agree

**Fact.** A replication of the NIH review process: **43 oncology researchers**, **25 real NIH R01 applications**, **four review panels** of 8–12 reviewers, 83 primary-reviewer ratings and critiques. Agreement between reviewers evaluating the *same* application was **ICC = 0** for overall ratings (95% CI 0–0.14), 0 for strengths, 0.017 for weaknesses; Krippendorff's alpha near zero throughout.
**Source.** Pier et al., "Low agreement among reviewers evaluating the same NIH grant applications," PNAS — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1714379115
**Published.** 2018-03-05 `[direct]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The strongest single argument for a written rubric with per-criterion scores: without one, two competent experts reading the identical application produce essentially uncorrelated verdicts. "Send it to a second partner" is not a calibration mechanism.

## 9. Order effects: your score depends on who you followed

**Fact.** Ten years of MBA admission interviews: **9,323 interviews by 31 interviewers**, averaging **4.5 interviews per day**. Scores are negatively autocorrelated within a day (B = −0.116, p = .005). A **one-SD increase (0.75) in the average score given to earlier applicants that day lowers the next applicant's expected score by ~0.075** — equivalent to about a third of a one-SD swing in the communication subscore. The effect grows with serial position and is stronger after a run of identical scores (B = −0.111 vs −0.059).
**Source.** Simonsohn & Gino, "Daily Horizons: Evidence of Narrow Bracketing in Judgment from 10 Years of MBA-Admission Interviews," Psychological Science 24(2), 219–224 — https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2070623
**Published.** February 2013 `[direct — read from the Harvard DASH PDF]`
**How to use in the playbook.** Concrete rule to give readers: never rank inside a day's queue. Score against a fixed rubric, then rank the whole batch at once — because a strong deck read at 10am is silently taxing the deck read at 11am.

## 10. Sequential reviewers reverse themselves for no reason

**Fact.** Across three high-stakes settings, decisions are negatively autocorrelated in ways unrelated to case merit. **Loan officers are 8 percentage points less likely to approve** the current loan if they approved the previous one (**7.5 pp** after two approvals in a row). Asylum judges are up to **3.3 pp** more likely to reject after a grant (**5.5 pp** after a streak of two grants); baseline unconditional effect 0.5 pp. Umpires: **0.9–1.5 pp**. The bias is stronger for less experienced reviewers, after longer streaks, when cases are similar or close in time, and when incentives for accuracy are weak.
**Source.** Chen, Moskowitz & Shue, "Decision Making Under the Gambler's Fallacy," Quarterly Journal of Economics 131(3), 1181–1242 — https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/131/3/1181/2590011 (NBER WP 22026, February 2016)
**Published.** NBER WP February 2016; QJE 2016 `[direct — percentages read from the working-paper PDF]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The "queue position is a hidden criterion" section. Pairs with fact #9: #9 is contrast within a batch, #10 is streak-breaking across a queue. Both vanish if scoring is criterion-anchored and ranking happens once, at the end.

## 11. Mechanical aggregation beats holistic judgment

**Fact.** Meta-analysis of selection and admissions decisions: combining the same information **mechanically (formula/rubric) rather than holistically improves prediction of job performance by more than 50%**. The loss from holistic combination is consistent and substantial, and it persists **even among experts** who know the job and the organization well. The type of mechanical method matters far less than whether one is used at all.
**Source.** Kuncel, Klieger, Connelly & Ones, "Mechanical Versus Clinical Data Combination in Selection and Admissions Decisions: A Meta-Analysis," Journal of Applied Psychology — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24041118/ (popular version: HBR, "In Hiring, Algorithms Beat Instinct," May 2014, https://hbr.org/2014/05/in-hiring-algorithms-beat-instinct)
**Published.** November 2013 (JAP); HBR version May 2014 `[direct — abstract read from the PDF]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The methodological spine: humans should supply the judgments per criterion, and a formula should combine them into the rank. This is the exact split EvalLens defends — AI/structure prepares the analysis, the human decides.

## 12. Structured screening works — with a documented failure mode

**Fact.** 255 business angels investing through an angel platform were compared against a gradient-boosted-tree model predicting investment outcomes. **Investors outperformed the algorithm only when they had extensive investment experience and managed to suppress their cognitive biases**; otherwise the model won.
**Source.** Blohm, Antretter, Sirén, Grichnik & Wincent, "It's a Peoples Game, Isn't It?!", Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice 46(4), 1054–1091 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1042258720945206
**Published.** 2022 (ET&P vol. 46 issue 4) `[direct — abstract via EconPapers]`
**How to use in the playbook.** Honest framing for the shortlist step: structure is not a replacement for the best judge in the room, it is a replacement for the *average* judge on the twentieth deck of the day.

## 13. The caveat that keeps the article credible

**Fact.** Study of VCs adopting data technologies for pre-investment screening: they get better at avoiding failures **within the pool that resembles historical data**, tilt investment toward that pool, and become **less likely to back the innovative startups that produce rare major successes**. The paper argues the effects are causal.
**Source.** Maxime Bonelli, "Data-driven Investors" — working paper, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4362173 (September 23, 2024 draft: https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/private/2025-05/bonelli_m._2024-data-driven_investors.pdf)
**Published.** SSRN 2023, cited draft dated 2024-09-23 `[snippet + dated PDF]`
**Chronology note.** The journal version (Review of Financial Studies) appeared **after** May 2026 — cite the working paper only.
**How to use in the playbook.** The "what a ranked shortlist must not do" section: rank on evidence quality and criterion fit, keep an explicit outlier lane, and never let the model's comfort zone become the fund's thesis.

## 14. What the process actually looks like today

**Fact.** Survey of **275 private capital professionals** (VC, PE, growth, CVC, accelerators/incubators), fielded **22 Sept – 31 Oct 2025**: **more than half (57%) spend 21+ hours per week on deal research** (33% at 21–40 hours, 24% at 41–60). AI use for **investment decisions more than doubled, 13% → 28%** YoY. **50%** rank deal sourcing their #1 priority; **68%** expect higher deal volume; **47%** have consolidated to just 1–3 core data sources (down from the usual 4–6).
**Source.** Affinity, "2026 Private Capital Predictions" — https://www.affinity.co/blog/2026-private-capital-predictions ; report: https://www.affinity.co/report/affinity-predictions-report
**Published.** Blog dated 2026-02-12; survey fielded Sept–Oct 2025 `[direct]`
**How to use in the playbook.** The "where the week actually goes" opener for the process section: half a working week per investor spent on research, rising volume, and a tooling stack being consolidated rather than expanded. The playbook sells *fewer, better-ordered decisions*, not another tab.

## Facts deliberately NOT used

- Any "VC associates screen 30–40 decks a day / 90 seconds per deck" claim: found only on undated vendor and SEO blogs, no primary source. Do not cite.
- Precise YC per-batch application counts (25k / 96k / "0.6% acceptance"): circulating on LinkedIn posts and SEO sites, not officially published, and several trace to 2026-dated pages. Use the ~1% framing from fact #7 instead.
- Bonelli's Review of Financial Studies version, the 2026 Frontiers decision-fatigue review, and the tandfonline healthcare decision-fatigue systematic review (paywalled, date unverified) — dropped for chronology or verification reasons.
- Israeli parole-judges "65% → 0%" study: contested (case-ordering artifact). Facts #9 and #10 make the same point with cleaner identification.

## Suggested narrative spine

1. Volume framing — 101 considered per close (#5), ~1% accepted (#7), 923 full applications in one EU cut-off (#6).
2. Attention math — under 3 minutes per deck and falling (#2, #3), supply up (#3).
3. What breaks — no inter-reviewer agreement (#8), contrast effects within a day (#9), streak reversals in a queue (#10).
4. What fixes it — criterion-level scoring plus mechanical aggregation (#11), structure beats the average tired reviewer (#12), extract the four fields that actually get read (#4).
5. What to guard against — pattern-matching collapse toward the familiar (#13).
6. Where the week goes today, and what a ranked shortlist gives back (#14).
